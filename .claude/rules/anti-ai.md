# Anti-AI Writing Style

> The complete anti-AI reference. `qa-gate` applies this for the `anti_ai` check; every writing
> skill (commenting-others, respond-to-own, dm-sales-coach, post, lead-magnet-gen) reads it.
>
> **Scope.** This file targets tells a human LinkedIn reader pattern-matches on, not forensic
> AI-detector evasion. Per `deep-research-report.md` (a research survey of actual AI-text-detection
> methods), no single surface-level tell, perfect grammar, headings, punctuation, three-item lists,
> is reliable evidence of AI authorship on its own; real detection leans on statistical, watermark,
> and provenance signals this file has no access to and isn't trying to approximate. What surface
> tells *are* good for is exactly this file's job: avoiding the specific patterns that make a human
> reader's eyes catch on "this sounds like AI," which is a lower, more achievable bar than forensic
> proof, and the one that actually matters on LinkedIn.
>
> Rules below are organized by evidentiary weight, not by when they were added:
> - **Hard ban** — wrong on a single occurrence, regardless of what else is in the passage.
> - **Cluster tell** — weak alone; only worth flagging when several appear together.

---

## Never invent facts (highest-priority rule, outside the tier system)
No number, client situation, quote, name, or lived moment in a draft may be invented to make it
sound concrete. This is stricter than "manufactured vividness" below: even a plain, unpolished
made-up detail is banned, not just a poetic one. If a draft needs a real example and the owner
hasn't given one, either ask for it or write the plain version without it; don't fill the gap with
a plausible-sounding stand-in. Ties to CLAUDE.md's hard rule ("no fabricated lived detail") and to
`gates.md`'s compliance bucket rules for financial content, where an invented number or client
detail isn't just an AI tell, it's a compliance breach. This isn't a stylistic AI-tell at all, it's
a factual-integrity rule, which is why it sits above the tier system rather than inside it.

# HARD BANS (flag on a single occurrence)

> Without this, Claude writes like Claude. With it, Claude writes like the owner.

## Banned words (never use)
delve · harness · tapestry · leverage (as a verb) · seamlessly · elevate · robust ·
transformative · empower / empowerment · curate / curated · journey (growth metaphor) ·
pivot · game-changer · ecosystem (of a brand/career) · synergy · unlock (of potential) ·
navigate (generic) · thrive (generic) · holistic · impactful (say the actual impact) ·
spaces (e.g. "the coaching space") · actionable · resonate (overused) ·
authentic / authenticity (self-applied) · thought leader (self-applied) · disruptive ·
innovative (only with proof) · "in today's fast-paced world" · "at the end of the day"
(as transition) · paradigm shift · cutting-edge · crucial · pivotal · underscore · testament ·
showcase · foster · bolster · garner · profound · powerful (as filler) · vibrant · landscape (abstract)

## Banned phrases & sentence patterns
**Reversal / contrast tricks**
- **The AI pivot (catch the move, not just the wording):** "This isn't about X. It's about Y." Also any reworded reversal with the same setup → contrast → reframe shape, for example "The opportunities already existed. What didn't exist was one place to see them." Same trick in different clothes. If a sentence strips down to "[Thing] wasn't the problem. [Other thing] was," it's banned regardless of the words used. → make the point directly.
- **Difficulty-escalation reversal (a pivot without negation):** "X is easy. Y is the actual test." Concedes the first thing instead of denying it, then elevates the second as the "real" bar, same setup → contrast → reframe move as the AI pivot above, just without an "isn't/wasn't." Strip-down test: "[Thing] is easy. [Other thing] is what actually matters/counts/is hard." Banned regardless of which verb carries the concession.
- **Negative parallelism:** "not just X, but Y" / "It's not X. It's Y" in any phrasing. Defensive rhythm real people rarely use unprompted.
- **Symmetric quotable pair (the pivot's negation-free sibling):** two clauses built as a matched escalation or contrast with no "isn't/wasn't" anywhere. "Good X does A. Great X does B." "X matters. Y matters more." "X alone doesn't do A. A well-planned X can do B." Same engineered-for-quotability move as the AI pivot above, just without a negation word to catch it on. Strip-down test: if a sentence pair splits cleanly into two symmetric halves that could stand alone as a slogan, it's banned, regardless of whether negation is present. This one is easy to reintroduce by accident while fixing the AI pivot elsewhere in the same draft (it feels like a "sharp reframe," not a repeat of the thing you just removed) — check closing lines and any sentence that feels like "the quotable one" specifically for this shape.
  - **POSTS-ONLY EXCEPTION (owner decision, 2026-08-10): the named-role contrast is allowed in LinkedIn posts.** A contrast that names two roles or identities and gives each a concrete line it would actually say is the identity-shift move the whole `positioning.md` spine is built on ("from X → to Y"), and it's the strongest thing in the owner's own reference drafts. Allowed shape, posts only:
    > Worker: "I need to make sure this gets done."
    > Builder: "I need to design how this gets done consistently."
    Three conditions, all required: both halves name a **role/identity** (worker/builder, operator/architect, hero/builder), both halves are **concrete and speakable** (something a real person would say out loud, not an abstraction), and it appears **once per post**. The abstract version stays banned in posts too: "Your value is not measured by how much you personally carry" has no named role and nothing quotable, so it's still the engineered-for-quotability move this rule exists to catch. Comments and DMs keep the full ban — a role contrast in a comment reads as copy, per the Banned arcs section.

**Elevated-sounding constructions**
- **Copula avoidance:** swapping a plain "is/are" for "serves as, stands as, marks, represents, boasts, features, offers." Say "is."
- **False range:** "from X to Y, from A to B" where X/Y/A/B aren't actually points on one scale. Used to sound comprehensive without being specific.
- **Persuasive-authority filler:** "the real question is," "at its core," "in reality," "what really matters," "fundamentally," "the heart of the matter." These announce depth instead of adding it; cut straight to the ordinary point underneath.
- **Aphorism formula:** "X is the Y of Z," "X becomes a trap," "the language of...," "the currency of...," "the architecture of..." Sounds profound, says nothing specific. Replace with the concrete claim it's gesturing at.
- **Signposting / tutorial voice:** "Let's dive in," "here's what you need to know," "without further ado." Just say the thing.

**Openings and endings**
- **Authority announcement opening:** "As a [credential] with X years..." → never announce credentials; the insight is the credential.
- **Preamble that adds nothing:** "In this post I'm going to share..." → start with the thing.
- **Algorithm hook:** compressed buzzword strings ("Niche authority over broad noise"). No human talks like this.
- **Fake-candid opener:** a theatrical pause-and-reveal built on a casual word, e.g. "Is it worth it? Honestly? It depends." The tell is the structure (rhetorical question, one-word aside, then the "real" answer), not the word "honestly" itself, which is fine used naturally mid-sentence (see Human texture).
- **Motivational-poster ending:** "Certainty calls. Answer it." → end with a specific insight or one clear action.

**Hollow content shapes**
- **Generic framework post:** "Here are 5 steps to X" with no real story behind it.
- **Both-sides non-opinion:** "Some believe X, others Y. It depends." → state your take (acknowledge, then redirect).
- **Fake vulnerability:** "I used to struggle, now I've figured it out, here's how." → real vulnerability is specific and still incomplete.
- **Manufactured punchlines / staccato drama:** a run of short declarative fragments stacked for effect ("No aesthetic prior. No nostalgia for human taste. The old rules were gone."). One short sentence for emphasis is fine; three or four in a row is engineered drama.
  - **POSTS-ONLY EXCEPTION (owner decision, 2026-08-10): the symptom stack is allowed in LinkedIn posts.** A run of 3–4 short lines is allowed when **every line carries a distinct, concrete specific the reader can check against her own week**. This is `voice-profile.md`'s example-stacking and `kk-post.md`'s "short, accumulative lines, then a hard stop," and it's what makes a post's opening a mirror instead of a claim. Allowed:
    > Every task waits for your approval.
    > Every client question returns to your inbox.
    > Every problem becomes your problem.
    Each line names a different real thing. Still banned, in posts as everywhere: a stack where the lines carry **rhythm instead of content** — "The business looks busy from the outside." / "The problem is structural." / "You call it growth." Those are drum beats, not symptoms. **The test:** could the reader answer "yes, that's me" or "no, not me" to each line separately? If yes, it's a symptom stack (allowed). If the line is a narrator's comment on the situation rather than a fact about her business, it's staccato drama (banned). Max one stack per post. Comments and DMs keep the full ban.

**Fragments and residue**
- **Tailing negation:** a clipped "no guessing," "no wasted motion," "no fluff" tacked onto the end of a sentence instead of written as a real clause. Write the actual clause: "so you're not left guessing."
- **Subjectless fragment / stealth passive:** "No configuration needed," "Results are saved automatically." Say who's doing what: "You don't need to set anything up," "It saves your results automatically."
- **Chatbot residue:** "I hope this helps," "Great question!," "You're absolutely right," "as of [date] / based on available information" — correspondence artifacts that have no business in a comment, DM, or post.

## Banned formatting
- Bullets for *ideas* (bullets are for task lists; ideas flow in paragraphs). **Unchanged: literal bullet characters stay banned everywhere.** The posts-only symptom-stack exception above is about line breaks, not bullets — the lines are plain sentences on their own lines, never prefixed with •, -, or a number.
- One-line paragraphs for emphasis (only when the pause truly serves). **Posts-only relaxation (2026-08-10):** in LinkedIn posts, standing a line alone is normal platform formatting rather than engineered emphasis, so this is judged by the symptom-stack test above (does the line carry a checkable specific) rather than banned on sight. Comments and DMs keep the original rule.
- Emoji in titles/headers.
- Summary sections at the end (trust the reader).
- **Em dashes or hyphens within sentences** — restructure instead. Period, comma, or semicolon. This is the #1 AI tell.
- **Curly/smart quotes** ( " " ' ' ) — use straight quotes.
- **Uniform compound-word hyphenation:** always hyphenating "third-party," "data-driven," "real-time," "long-term," etc., including in predicate position ("the report is high-quality"). Humans hyphenate inconsistently: keep the hyphen when the compound sits before the noun ("a high-quality report"), drop it after ("the report is high quality").

## Banned arcs (whole passage, not just one sentence or line)
> A passage can pass every word- and formatting-level check above and still read as AI, because
> the *shape of the whole thing* is the tell. Check the arc, not just the lines.

- **The comment shape, the actual target:** one reaction + one personal observation. No lesson
  required. Write like the owner typed the thought after reading the post once: ordinary words, a
  slightly uneven sentence structure, done in two moves, not four. Inside that reaction, avoid
  contrast constructions ("not X, but Y," already banned above under Negative parallelism/AI
  pivot, worth restating here because it's the most common way a comment quietly turns into copy),
  extended metaphors, three-part rhetorical lists (see Rule of three, Cluster tells below),
  summarising the author's argument back to them (they wrote the post; they know what it said),
  and a polished closing aphorism (see Motivational-poster ending). Don't manufacture profundity
  where a plain reaction would do.
- **Opening every post with a question.**
- **10x/100x claims** without proof.
- **Brag disguised as a lesson.**
- **A closing CTA on every single post.**
- **The mini-post arc (what the comment shape rule above guards against):** reference their idea →
  reframe it → analogy or your own experience → landed insight, packed into 2-4 sentences. If a
  comment has a setup, a turn, and a landed lesson, it reads like copy, even if none of the
  individual sentences trip a ban above. Cut it down to the one reaction plus one observation.
- **The self-promotion pivot (comments especially):** turning someone else's post into a mirror for
  your own expertise, e.g. "same gap I keep finding inside businesses," "this is exactly the
  pattern I see with clients." It's fine to relate their point to something you know, but if the
  sentence would work unchanged on 50 other people's posts, it's not a response to *them*, it's
  positioning wearing a response's clothes. Ties to voice.md's rule: comments must be specific to
  this person, never copy-paste-able.
- **Manufactured vividness:** a concrete detail that's suspiciously well-formed: rhythmic,
  alliterative, or too neatly constructed ("scattered across ten inboxes and one person's memory").
  Real recalled specifics have rough edges, an odd number, an unglamorous noun, something that
  doesn't quite scan as a line. If a detail sounds like it was written for effect rather than
  remembered, it was. That polish is itself an AI tell, not proof the writing is "concrete enough."
  Prefer a plainer real detail over an invented vivid one.

# CLUSTER TELLS (flag only when 2+ co-occur in the same passage)

> `deep-research-report.md`'s central finding, backed by real detector benchmarks: surface-level
> tells like these are weak evidence when isolated and are shared by plenty of legitimate human
> writing. They only become meaningful when several show up together in the same passage. Sanding
> every draft down over one isolated hit destroys the human texture voice.md asks for.

### Not a tell at all (never flag, regardless of context)
- **Casual markers used naturally.** "Honestly," "look," and "here's the thing" mid-sentence are
  exactly what Human texture calls for. Only the theatrical question-pause-reveal *structure*
  built around them is a Hard ban (see Fake-candid opener above), the words themselves are fine.
- **One short emphatic sentence.** Only a run of three or more in a row is staccato drama (already
  a Hard ban above, with its own threshold).
- **Perfect grammar**, and a mixed casual/formal register in the same message.
- **A short "…" mid-thought or a sentence fragment**, per Human texture's bounded allowance.

### Weak alone; flag only when 2+ co-occur
- **Rule of three** — grouping items in threes to seem comprehensive.
- **Elegant variation** — awkward synonyms to avoid repeating a name/term.
- **Vague attribution** — "industry reports, researchers argue, experts suggest" with no specific source.
- **Notability/attribution emphasis** — listing sources/media coverage to assert importance without summarising what they said.
- **Superficial analysis** — vague present-participle commentary about significance and impact; unattributed opinion dressed as analysis.
- **Promotional language** — travel-guide/press-release tone: "vibrant, nestled, enchanting."
- **Inflated significance / legacy claims** — "stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, plays a vital/crucial/pivotal role, underscores its importance, reflects broader, symbolizing its enduring/lasting, setting the stage for, marks a shift, evolving landscape, indelible mark, deeply rooted." Puffs up importance even for mundane subjects.
- **AI vocabulary at disproportionate frequency** — additionally, crucial, delve, intricate, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, vibrant. (A single instance of one of these is already a Hard ban per Banned words above; this entry is about frequency/clustering of *near-miss* vocabulary that isn't already on that list.)
- **Formatting tells** — excessive boldface; inline-header lists ("**Term:** description"); Title Case In Every Heading.
- **Outline-like conclusions** — "Despite its strengths, X faces challenges..." then speculation.
- **Human indicators (the positive counter-signal)** — specific, unusual, nuanced facts; natural reasoning for stylistic choices; specificity over generic positivity. Their presence weighs *against* flagging a cluster, even if a couple of the tells above are present.

### Two hard exceptions run the other way
Em dashes and curly quotes are banned categorically, as house style (see Banned formatting above),
regardless of clustering. Unlike the general research finding that "em dashes alone aren't proof,"
this workspace bans them outright per the owner's stated preference.

### Caveats
Detection tools have real error rates; not all text with these signs is AI; these are signs of a
potential problem, not proof; and detector benchmarks (RAID, MAGE, M4, cited in
`deep-research-report.md`) show these surface regularities are unstable across domains and models,
which is exactly why they're gated behind clustering here rather than enforced individually.

## What good (human) writing sounds like
- **Simple English** — clear, not polished-to-impress. The word that fits gets used.
- **Real examples always** — lived experience, an (anonymised) client situation, a real number. Something AI can't generate. Without it, hollow.
- **Acknowledgment pivot** — "I agree, however here's what's actually happening."
- **Big picture first, then specific.**
- **Opening line** — a sharp, bold claim that creates a question in the reader's mind without asking one.
- **Closing** — a specific insight or one clear action, not a rhetorical question.

## Human texture (close the gap to how a person actually talks)
> The positive counterpart to the bans above: what makes writing read like one person speaking to
> one person, not a broadcast. Apply on top of everything else; where a texture move would break a
> Hard ban (em dash, vague trailing ending, opening every post with a question), the ban wins.
- **Start sentences with "And" or "But"** when it fits the rhythm. Real thinking connects that way.
- **Casual markers, in moderation** — "honestly," "look," "here's what I mean," "think about it this way," "you know that moment when." Sprinkle, don't saturate. Use them as they'd naturally fall in a sentence, not as a standalone rhetorical-question-then-one-word-aside opener (that structure is a Hard ban, see Banned phrases & sentence patterns).
- **Admit real uncertainty** — "maybe it's just me, but," "I'm not sure yet, and that's the point." Only where it's true; never fake humility.
- **Open on a moment of recognition,** not a lecture or a credential. Name the feeling the reader already has, then go deeper. (The sharp-claim opening still works; this is the warmer sibling.)
- **Insight as discovery, not declaration** — let the point land like it was just worked out, not announced from a stage.
- **Show the lived detail, don't narrate having lived it** (owner correction, 2026-08-10). Every writing
  skill requires at least one line that's unmistakably the owner's — a real first-person scene or a
  signature lens. The failure mode isn't leaving it out. It's *announcing* it: "I spent years in a role
  where…", "having worked in…", "in my experience…". That's a credential preamble wearing a story's
  clothes, and it trips the same wire as the Authority announcement opening (Hard ban above) — the
  reader shifts from feeling to evaluating, which is exactly why the owner stopped announcing
  credentials at all (`about-me.md` → Story bank).
  **The move:** write the scene precisely enough that only someone who lived it could have written it,
  and let that carry the authority. Drop the setup clause entirely.
  - Narrated: "I spent years in a role where I was positioned to make decisions and watched them get overwritten."
  - Shown: "You can be the person expected to decide, and still watch the decision get overwritten by someone who couldn't make it themselves, and still wait a year for it to move."
  Same true detail, no résumé. The specificity is the proof.
  **The test:** delete the first clause. If the sentence still lands, the clause was announcing, not
  telling. **The bound:** this is about dropping the *setup*, not about hiding behind a generic "you."
  A shown line still has to carry a checkable specific. If removing the "I" leaves a general
  observation any thoughtful stranger could write, the line has failed the lived-detail rule, not
  passed it. And where a real number or scene could be misread as the *target's* rather than the
  owner's, mark it as hers explicitly — `commenting-others`' misattribution guard still wins.
- **Write to one real person** on the other side of the table, never an audience.
- **Concrete over abstract, always** — "land the client," "sleep through the night," "one employer, one decision and it's gone." Never "achieve success" or "mitigate risk."
- **Allowed, but bounded** — an occasional sentence fragment for emphasis when the pause truly serves, and "…" mid-thought to show the mind moving. The piece still CLOSES on a firm, specific point, never a trailing "…" or a vague motivational line.
- **Banned filler phrasing** — "in order to" (use "to"), "due to the fact that" (use "because"), "it is important to note" (just note it), "one might consider" (use "you might").

The owner's specific voice, examples, and signature phrases come from `.claude/rules/voice.md`
(outreach) and `kk-post.md` (posts). This file says what to avoid; those say what to sound like.

## The litmus test
> "Does this sound like something the owner would actually say — or like an AI trying hard to imitate them? Could someone get the same answer from ChatGPT?"

Three things that matter most:
1. Lived personal experience — without it, it isn't theirs.
2. Real examples stacked from different angles until the point is undeniable.
3. Never announce authority — drop the sharp insight and let it carry.
